Internal (skills/process): a PR's *What's asked of you* now carries only the decisions the product owner actually owns.

**The defect.** The layout was right and the register was wrong. A spec PR reached the user with a six-row decision table in which one row was the mechanism the section above had already described, one was a pure implementation call ("feed the new lenses from the shared evidence bundle rather than wiring data to each one"), and one was an obviously-correct choice that cost nothing to reverse — all typeset identically to the two rows that genuinely changed what a reader of our reports would see. Below it sat a ~450-word live fork and three dense paragraphs of engineering prose, with no marker anywhere saying where the product owner's job ended. The result is a reader who finishes six rows unable to name which one was theirs, which is a more expensive failure than a long description: it converts a decision into a rubber stamp.

Two rules were feeding it. `pr-reviewer-guidance.md` §3 policed the *cost* column ("write it in consequences, not mechanism") but never the Decision column itself, so an implementation call with a well-written cost cell passed. And "where the artifact defines a sign-off surface, the cap doesn't apply — show all of it" was read as licence to index all eight of a spec's §6 rows onto the surface a product owner reads, even though `writing-for-humans.md` already carried the sharper test — is the reader being asked to *decide* on this row, or merely *informed* of it.

**The fix — sort by audience, don't lengthen or drop.** §3 gains two filters a row must pass. **Is it theirs?** A wrong answer has to change one of four things: what a user experiences, what we've promised anyone outside the team, when something ships, or what it costs to undo. **Is it worth their attention?** A call that is obviously right or reversible at near-zero cost is one you make and note, because surfacing it *as* a decision implies weight it doesn't have and spends the attention the row beside it needed. Plus the rule that catches the most inflation: a row describing what the change *does* belongs to block 2, and re-listing the mechanism as decisions is what turns a two-decision change into a six-row table. **Three rows is the ceiling, hardest first**, and a live fork is capped at one to three sentences per part against the ~200-word budget that already existed.

Everything filtered out lands in a new collapsed **engineering calls** block — one line each, in the engineer's register, where the code reviewer wants it anyway. That block is what makes the filters safe on a spec PR, where §6 is what approval certifies: nothing is dropped from the record and nothing is approved unseen, it is sorted by who it is addressed to. A §6 that yields six product rows is a §6 that put the owner in the engineer's chair, and the fix is upstream in the spec rather than a longer table in the PR.

**The audience boundary is now explicit.** The layout table gains a *For* column, and block 4 (*Parts worth reviewing closely*) opens by saying the audience has changed to the code reviewer — so a product owner stops at the end of block 3 instead of skimming three paragraphs of mechanism looking for another ask. A worked before/after runs a real six-row table through both filters and lands on one fork and two rows.

Propagated to the surfaces that author asks: `issue-spec` Step 6 sorts §6 instead of indexing it wholesale and flags an over-three table as an upstream §6 defect; `issue-implement` Step 9 sorts the Key Decisions list rather than reusing the top five verbatim; `epic-agent` applies the same filters to cross-cutting decisions; `spec-template.md` §6 adopts the four-part test and names the two rows most often mistaken for Decisions; `writing-for-humans.md` restates its enumeration rule as decide-vs-inform sorting; BP-041 and `asking-for-decisions.md` carry the filters as the gate on whether an ask reaches the user at all.
