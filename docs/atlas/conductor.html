Living design

Drive this project with the thing we're building

Conductor is one effort: automate the SDLC, starting with issues and epics, later the PRD and the roadmap. It is a meta-harness — an orchestration tool that drives other coding harnesses. Its central bet is that the session you talk to stays small on purpose: the work lives in resources and the jobs are split across roles that are blocks, so the coordinator only ever reasons over what is open right now.

01

What Conductor is

A harness that drives harnesses. It opens a coding agent, gives it a job with the context that job needs, watches what comes back, and decides what happens next. Claude Code is the harness on the other end most of the time. Conductor never writes the code.

Now
issues and epics

One tool that takes an objective, breaks it into issues, drives each through spec, implementation and review, and stops where a person has to decide.

Next
prd and roadmap

The same substrate holding why something is being built, not only what. Roadmapping becomes a read of state rather than a document that rots.

Eventually
the oracle

A central place to ask what is being built and why, answered from what the system actually holds instead of from a person's memory of the last six weeks.

Never
the thing writing code

Conductor assembles context, dispatches, watches and routes. Editing files is the harness's job, and holding that line is what lets the harness be swapped.

The coordinator stays small on purpose

The session you talk to is the coordinator. Its job on any given wake is narrow: it knows which questions are outstanding, it classifies your reply against that set, and it routes the answer to the workstream that asked. It does not hold the epic, the specs, the diffs, or any workstream's transcript — because the outstanding-question set lives in a resource, not in the conversation.

That is the architectural bet, and everything downstream is built to keep it true. A coordinator whose context grows with the run degrades with the run. A coordinator whose context is the currently open questions is the same size on day four as on day one, no matter how much work happened in between. State in a resource, not in context.

Why this is not today's process with extra steps

The epic-em skill already plays the coordinator role — it dispatches workers, surfaces gates, and holds a status table. Two things it cannot do. It cannot stay bounded: everything it learns accumulates in one transcript, so a long epic and a rotting context are the same event. And it cannot separate the jobs: coordinating, talking to you, planning the architecture and managing the workstreams all happen in that one context. Conductor's coordinator is bounded by construction — section 6 is where that construction lives — and narrow by role, which is the rest of this section.

A session is owned by a workflow, not by an agent

This is the sharpest difference between Conductor and the harnesses it drives, and it needs stating narrowly or it is wrong. Harnesses do have sub-agents, and a sub-agent does get its own context, so the difference is not that isolated context is unavailable. The difference is which layer is allowed to have it. In Claude Code and the harnesses like it, the top layer belongs to one agent: that agent holds the plan, talks to you, and routes your next reply, and its context grows for as long as the run does. A sub-agent can be handed a job and hand back a summary. It cannot take over the conversation, hold the plan, or route your reply — so the one context that must not rot is exactly the one that cannot be split.

FSD sessions are not shaped that way. A session record carries a flowKind — it belongs to the workflow — and a workflow can take on several isolated roles at once, inside the same request. A role is a block, and each role block declares its own context and its own tools. Isolation comes from the block boundary, not from a session boundary, so one request can run three roles without any of them seeing the others' working set — and the coordinator is one of those roles rather than the container the rest hang beneath.

So read the claim above precisely, because the flattering reading is the wrong one. The coordinator is not holding the epic, the specs and the workstreams and exercising restraint about what it looks at. It delegates to role blocks that each hold their own narrow context, and its own job — classify a reply, route it — stays small because there is nothing else in it.

Three jobs, three roles

The skill-based process we run today puts all of it in one place. One top-level agent coordinates the work, talks to you, keeps the architecture coherent, and manages the workstreams. Those are separate jobs, and running them in one context is what makes a long epic degrade — so they should be separate roles.

Coordinator
owns the conversation

Works out who needs to be addressed, routes the conversation, and translates work the other roles did back into terms you can act on. Conversation routing, not planning.

Manager
owns the work · same session

Reads the board and pushes. Is anything stalled, is a workstream not progressing, does the plan still fit. A block in the coordinator's own chain — a different role, not a different session.

Architect
consults · org-scoped

Does not design. Checks that final designs find alignment and cohesion, within an epic and across epics, and raises what it finds. No write authority over anybody's board.

The coordinator owns the conversation; the manager owns the work. Those two sit in the same session because their subject is the same epic, and the manager reaching the board through the coordinator's chain keeps the board single-writer — the contract note below is why that is a choice and not a constraint. Whether the manager may act on what it finds, or only report it, is genuinely open (thread 12).

The architect lands somewhere else, and for a principled reason rather than a taste: the manager's subject is one epic; the architect's spans many. A role whose whole value is seeing across epics cannot have its memory tied to one of them. Which is a claim about scope, and scope turns out to be a separate axis from the one the next heading is about.

A role is always a block. The call site and the scope are what vary

“Is this role a block or a workstream?” looks like an open question and is not one. Isolation is free in a block. Concurrency is what costs a session. Both a block and a workstream give a role its own context and its own tools, so isolation cannot be the thing that decides between them. What only a workstream adds is running concurrently with, or longer than, the coordinator's turn.

So a role is always a block, and the first question left is where it is called: invoked in-turn, or dispatched into a workstream that runs that same block. The architect is not either a block or a workstream. A quick cohesion check is in-turn; a sweep across every open spec is dispatched. Same role, same block, two call sites. “Workstream per role” is a false frame — it is workstream per long-running invocation.

The second question is scope, and it is orthogonal to the first. Agents and sessions are not bound together — an agent can be invoked into any session, and what decides what it knows is not where it runs but where its resources live. Put a role's memory in session-scoped resources and it knows this conversation. Put it in org-scoped resources and every session that invokes it gets the same role, seeing across everything. The agent travels; the memory stays put.

One constraint here is real and worth quoting exactly, because it is written into the only spawn verb there is — and because the inference usually drawn from it does not hold.

The contract, verbatim

startDetached creates a derived child session. Its own documentation: the child session key is derived from [principal, parent session, seed]; the child “inherits tenant, user, org and flow kind from the running request and records it as its parent”; and “there is no flow or action parameter, so flow.actions is not reachable.”

So a workstream is fixed in its parent's flow kind and cannot dispatch to an action. That much is a fact. The conclusion people draw from it — that a manager therefore cannot be a workstream — is not, because flow.actions is not the route to the board. A detached manager reaches the board the way any block does, through ctx.cap.<board>.addTask(…). That was run rather than reasoned about: the row landed in the durable store, and the same manager spawned a peer.

That makes the manager being a block in the coordinator's own chain a design choice, not a shape the framework forced. The choice is single-writer: one author per board transition, one place to look when a task is in a state nobody expects. Section 6 makes the same call for workstreams generally, and thread 5 holds it open rather than closing it.

Where the expensive thinking goes

The call-site question carries a cost position with it, and it is the design's stance rather than a preference. Conductor's own thinking should be cheap and structural — routing, deciding, planning. If a role needs hours of research, that work belongs in a harness run, which is already built for long autonomous work and already manages its own context. Hours of Conductor-side thinking is not a role working hard. It is the coordinator-stays-small principle failing quietly, one long turn at a time.

The architect is an org-scoped agent, not a session of its own

Apply the scope rule to the role that most needs it. The architect's memory goes in org-scoped resources, and then any session that consults it gets an architect that has seen every epic. It needs no session of its own and should not have one: a session is where a conversation lives, and the architect is not in a conversation with anybody. It is consulted.

Most of that is supported today, which is the useful half of the claim.

What the architect is for

It does not design. When a feature is being specced, the harness does that work, at that point in the flow, with the code in front of it. The architect is a consultant and a monitor: it checks that final designs find alignment and cohesion — within an epic, and across epics. That is a narrower job than “owns the architecture,” and it is the job that actually needs cross-epic sight.

It has no write authority over any epic's board. It raises; the coordinator decides. That is the same request-don't-mutate rule section 6 states for workstreams, applied to a role sitting above the board rather than inside it, and for the same reason: a board with one writer is a board you can reason about.

The architect may hold a task board of its own, org-scoped like the rest of it. proposed Two instances of the architect running in two sessions is not a contention problem to design around — it is a board working exactly as designed, since a board exists to be claimed from by N workers. The argument for giving it one is not concurrency at all. It is deduplication: two sessions consulting the architect about the same epic produce one claimed check, not two.

The agent surface is thin, and this would be its first real use

The shape is right and the machinery is unfinished, so read every role claim above as a design on a young surface rather than as assembly of a working one. Agent (packages/core/src/types/agent.ts) has no resources field and no notion of scope — resources reach an agent only through usesCapabilities. createWorkforceCapability is a stub: a // TODO returning a bare capability. usesSkills is marked RESERVED and nothing resolves it, and contextMode documents that only "inline" is honored.

And almost nothing exercises it. Exactly one place does: examples/guides/research-team staffs a skill seat through defineAgent + createAgentRegistry + materializeAgent, and has a test — with an inline persona, session scope and a single flow, which is none of the three things this design leans on. agentBlock and definePersona are used nowhere outside the workforce package. apps/kitchen-sink declares @flow-state-dev/workforce as a dependency and uses it in zero source files, and no integration test touches it. There is no existing example of an org-scoped agent, of an agent carrying resources, or of one agent used across two flows.

Code and docs also disagree on precisely the point this design leans on. apps/docs/docs/orchestration/agents.md states “There’s no cross-flow assignment.” A probe built two flows off one agent and it compiled — which is a green build, not a green runtime. Recorded here as an unresolved contradiction, not as evidence either way.

02

How we'll know it works

One criterion, three clauses, each falsifiable on its own.

ClauseIt fails if
We drive this project's development through the toolWe reach for a plain Claude session instead, for any real epic
The UX beats juggling Claude mega sessionsAn operator who has both prefers the manual route
It drives multi-day epics without degrading from context rotDay-three output on comparable work is worse than day one

The third clause is the one the design answers directly, and section 1 is the answer. Context rot is not a model problem to wait out; it is a consequence of putting the run's memory in the run's conversation. Conductor moves that memory into collections and leaves the coordinator a classification job whose input size is bounded by the number of open questions — typically one to three, never the whole epic.

The first two clauses are claims about a person's experience of doing real work. They are easy to fail and hard to fake, which is what makes them worth having, and expensive to measure, which is why measuring them is still open (section 12).

03

What a person does

One product owner, one objective, three gates. Everything between the gates is automated; a gate is a person reading a paragraph and replying.

code — deterministic, executes prose — a model reads markdown and follows it human — a person must act

Gates are not the only time a person is needed. A running agent also asks things — a spec decision it cannot derive, a permission, an ambiguity in the issue. Those arrive unpredictably, from any workstream, and they are the subject of section 6.

Two rules decide which parts of this loop become code. A rule that must not be half-remembered — a review budget, a feedback cap, dependency ordering — belongs in something that executes. A decision priced in customers, promises or timing stays human, and gets somewhere durable to live rather than a message in a chat window.

Who owns what

Product owner
human · all three gates

Objectives, roadmap, and anything priced in customers or promises. Spec approval is theirs alone — the coordinator never grants it.

Coordinator
classifies and routes

Works the inbox, surfaces gates, and translates the other roles' work back to you. Holds no epic in its head, and does no planning.

Architect and manager
two more roles, two placements

Role blocks with their own context and tools (section 1). The manager is a block in the coordinator's own chain — same session, same epic — and pushes on the running work. The architect is org-scoped: invoked into any session, it consults on cohesion and raises what it finds, and writes to nobody's board.

EM / PM postures
absorb decisions, move no gate

EM absorbs engineering forks. PM may cut scope inside an approved objective but never add — additions always go back to the owner.

Workstreams
bounded, disposable

One harness run per issue-phase, in its own session and its own worktree. Each returns at most a screen; the transcript stays where it ran.

04

The conductor flow

The lab that ships is one flow, five actions, one sequencer, one board per epic, one harness manager behind implement. Solid borders exist today. Dashed borders are the later design — startEpic, approve, classify-and-route — plus spec and review phases. steer is the talk door: the coordinator sees the board and may call seed / wake / answer. It does not implement, and it is not yet the classify-and-route flow in section 6.

Where a gate lives

ctx.suspend(options) durably pauses a flow pending external human action: request status becomes "suspended", a SuspensionRecord persists the whole context (suspensionId, requestId, flowKind, actionName, sessionId, userId, reason, message, data, resumeSchema, render, allow, status, blockInstanceId, stepIndex), and an HTTP resume route re-enters the action with completed steps replayed from the durable log rather than re-executed. reason: "human_approval" defaults allow to ["approve","reject"]; "human_input" to ["submit"]. It needs durable: true on the action and on the runtime. Four working shapes live in apps/kitchen-sink/flows/chat-agent/: requestApproval, askQuestion, collectForm, chooseOption.

All of that belongs to the main session, and it stays exactly as described. It is not the mechanism for anything a workstream is involved in — and today nothing stops one reaching for it anyway. A workstream that calls ctx.suspend gets a suspension: its request parks, the row reads as a job still going, and nobody upstream is told. A fence making the call an error inside a workstream is decided and filed. proposed It is not built.

Which reaches further than the detached case. Take the three gates of section 3. Two of them — a spec waiting on approval, a PR waiting on a merge — are raised after the workstream that did the work has ended, so there is no request left to suspend by the time anyone is asked. The third could suspend and does not, because running one gate on a different mechanism from the other two buys nothing and costs a second thing to reason about. What waits in all three cases is the task row, parked, and approve releases it. Nothing is given up by dropping the suspension: a parked row is durable state, so it survives a restart the same way a suspension record does, and it survives the run that created it too. The next section is that mechanism.

05

Starting an issue

Starting an issue is not a step. It is its own small flow, and most of it is filesystem work that happens before a single token is spent.

A claimed task row says "implement ISSUE-A". Between that row and a Claude Code run there is a working directory that has to exist, be on the right branch, and be nobody else's. That is the part a prompt cannot do for you, and the part that fails first.

06

Questions, in and out

The load-bearing subsystem. A detached workstream cannot stop and ask you something — so a question travels as data, and an answer travels back the same way.

Start with the constraint, because it is why this section exists. ctx.suspend pauses the request that calls it. A detached worker's parent has already returned — the row is in_progress and the parent's terminationReason is "handed-off". If a workstream suspends, the workstream pauses and nothing else does. You are never told. The epic keeps running around a run that is quietly waiting for a person who does not know they are needed.

And nothing refuses the call, which is the half worth saying out loud rather than assuming. A fence making ctx.suspend an error inside a workstream is decided and filed proposed — it is not built, and section 11 is where it sits. Until it lands the wrong mechanism stays reachable and fails quietly, which is an argument for building the right one rather than for waiting on the fence.

Detached closes session state, not resources

detached: true on claudeCodeAgent suppresses three things at once, and the option's own doc explains why they are one decision rather than three: the sessionStateSchema declaration, the reads and writes that go with it, and the SDK resume — “the session provider is not consulted at all.” Every one of those three is ctx.session.state or ctx.session.patchState.

Read why the flag is set, though, because it is easy to mistake for a stance. It is set so the board will construct at all: a detached worker may declare no sessionStateSchema anywhere in its tree, and claudeCodeAgent authors one unless the flag is on. A construction shim. It is not a decision that every run starts fresh, and it is not a bet that runs cannot be resumed.

Resources are a separate channel, and it stays open. The same block declares three session-scoped collections under recordWork and writes them from a detached run. The inbox is how a workstream asks a question, and it travels a channel detached leaves open, which is why asking needs no guard narrowed. Continuity is not a resource at all — it rides the task, below — and it does not travel that channel either.

Out — the inbox resource

A user-scoped collection, one instance per open question. The scope choice is load-bearing: a detached child inherits tenant, user, org and flow kind from the request that started it, so a user-scoped collection is one instance set shared by the coordinator and every workstream under it. A session-scoped collection would need sharedToWorkstream: true to reach the lineage at all, and would then be tied to one epic's session — the wrong lifetime for a thing you answer tomorrow morning. The run index further down makes the opposite call, for the opposite reason.

The board partitions by epic; the inbox does not. A board is a claim pool, and one collection id is one pool — the claim predicate tests status and deps only, so two epics sharing an id claim each other's rows (section 4). An inbox is not a pool: nothing claims a row, and issue ids are globally unique, so two epics cannot collide on a key. An unfiltered list() spanning every epic is the product rather than a defect — it is your inbox, and everything waiting on you sits in one place.

A coordinator-attached client picks a workstream's row up on its next entry, through the snapshot read.

A workstream requests a plan change; it does not make one

A run that discovers the plan is wrong — this issue needs splitting, that dependency points the wrong way, this task should not exist — has the board within reach. It should not touch it. It posts a plan-change request the same way it posts a question, and the top session decides what to do with it. proposed

The reason is single-writer. A board only one session writes is a board you can reason about: every transition has one author, and “why is this task pending” has one place to look. Let N workstreams edit the plan they are being run by and the board stops being a plan and becomes a negotiation, with no one holding the whole of it. The restriction also costs close to nothing once addressed messaging exists: the request travels the channel a mutation would have travelled anyway, and it arrives somewhere that can weigh it against the other seven issues rather than only against its own. Whether some workstreams should be trusted to write directly is carried as thread 5, not settled here.

Signal and content are separate

The question and the wake are two different things, and keeping them apart is what makes the wake cheap to build. The content — what was asked, by which workstream, against which task — is the inbox/** row. It is durable, it is re-readable, and it is the truth. The signal carries nothing but there is something for you. It is ephemeral, and it is an optimization over polling rather than a delivery guarantee.

The failure mode is the point. If the coordinator is down when the signal fires, the wake is lost — and nothing else is, because the row is still sitting in the collection and the next re-entry finds it. So the signal never has to be durable, and that removes most of what makes an event bus expensive: no persisted queue, no redelivery, no dead-letter, no acknowledgement protocol. A dropped wake costs latency.

How the coordinator wakes

Honestly: not by a resource hook. reactTo is real — a collection can bind a block to created / stateUpdated / deleted / contentUpdated — but the engine runs a reactive block in-session, inline in the mutating turn. A workstream writing an inbox row fires the hook inside the workstream's own request. It cannot reach the coordinator's session. Designing around a hook that quietly fires in the wrong place is the class of mistake this document exists to catch.

Two situations wear the same name. Hot is a wake while work is in flight: something is already looping, so the only question is how it notices a new row. Cold is a wake when nothing is running at all — the board drained, every workstream settled, and the epic waiting on a person who may take until tomorrow. Nothing is looping, so something outside has to re-enter. The useful thing about the split is that one answer covers both, and it is a watch.

Nothing is held open, and nothing polls

The blocking wait is tempting because it costs nothing to build. It is the wrong shape for the workload this project exists to survive. Holding a worker slot and a live loop open for the hours — or days — a person takes to answer means the machinery carrying a multi-day epic is a loop that must not die for a multi-day epic. Anything that restarts, redeploys, or trips a lease inside that window takes the run with it. A design that leans on an idle poll has quietly made the poll interval its liveness guarantee, which is not a guarantee.

So the intended path is quieter than that. A workstream blocked on a question ends its request, carrying the question out with it. Its task stays on the board, parked. The coordinator is not looping either — it holds a watch, and a watch costs a row. Nothing has to drain to nothing, because the board is the durable state: when the next request arrives, hopefully the answer, it picks that task back up and continues from there. Half of that is shipped and half is not, and the note below separates them. awaiting_review stays as the short-horizon convenience for “the operator is right here, answering now” — not as the thing the design depends on. What is left to design carefully is what re-enters, and how it finds the right outstanding row. The rest of this section is the answer; section 12 carries what it leaves to a separate build. Read it as the shape the first real run ships on rather than as a later upgrade to it. The Proof is relay plus a parked task row, and the short-horizon loop is not a stepping stone toward that — section 11 is why.

The gap the board had, and the three names that come with it

Checked in packages/orchestration/src/task-board/blocks/check-board.ts. awaiting_review is the blocking wait, and says so: it “keeps the loop alive in both modes — the worker's preceding .waitForCondition blocks until an external actor transitions the task back to pending.” The request stays open, which is precisely what the cold path is trying not to do. Everything else in the status set is terminal (completed, errored, cancelled) or out of reach from a claimed row: the state machine admits blocked only from pending, and it means a dependency is not met, not a person is. Leaving the row in_progress and ending the request is worse than either — it is indistinguishable from a child still working.

So the cold path needs two things the board did not have: a way for a parked task to let its request end, and then a way for that row to be picked back up. Which is exactly enough on a board that holds, and not enough on one that exits. Hold the drain and the loop is still turning when the row re-pends, so the next iteration picks it up; that case works today and this changes nothing about it. The coordinator never does the work, so it has no business claiming anything. What it wants is to know when a workstream's task settles, so it can route the result. That is a watch, and today there is no way to say it — which is most of why the wake looked like a hard problem. The only relationship on offer was the wrong one.

A watch is mail with a trigger. “When this row goes terminal, send me a message.” That framing is what makes it cheap, because it is not a new subsystem: addressing, delivery and arbitration on the session key all belong to the message layer described next, and a watch adds only the condition that decides when to send. Each watch names one recipient, so a task with three watchers settles into three addressed messages — three sends, not one broadcast. Fan-out stays ruled out, for the reason the next section gives.

Two design points, both settled.

Watches are keyed rows, one per (watcher, task) — not a list on the task row. A list is read-modify-write on one row by many writers, and the losers' writes vanish. That is the same contention this document rules out for the run index further down, for the same reason, and the fix is the same one: let the key carry the identity and let the collection do what a collection is for. “Everyone watching this task” is a list(), not an array anybody rewrites.

The send is best-effort, inside the settle — the fenced recorders, where every settle already goes. Settle-then-send can half-fail: the row goes terminal and the message never leaves. That is acceptable here, and it is acceptable because of the durability split two headings up rather than because of any care taken in the code. The message is ephemeral; the row is the truth. A lost send costs a wake, not a fact — the watcher re-reads the board on its next entry and catches up on everything it missed. So there is nothing here to engineer around. A two-phase commit between a task row and a message would buy latency in exchange for closing a failure mode the design already absorbs.

The wake is mail, not a broadcast

The reason no existing package covers the cold-path wake is worth stating precisely rather than filing as a gap. An inbound transport carries external origin into a flow: mcp, chat-sdk, voice-openai and scheduled each translate somebody else's protocol into a dispatch and stamp where it came from. node and bullmq are host and runtime adapters — they serve the process and run the jobs, and receive nothing on their own account. A workstream telling a coordinator it has a question is neither of those. It is session-to-session, inside one system, under one principal. That is a different seam, and it is empty.

What fills it is mail: a message bus, not an event bus — a working name this document keeps for readability, though the name is now settled: the filed epic is called relay (thread 3, section 11). One message travels from one request to one named recipient — another session, or the same session as a new request. Sessions address each other directly, so a workstream reaches the coordinator, the coordinator reaches a workstream, and two top-level sessions reach each other the same way. There is no topic, no subscriber list, and no fan-out. What a sender may legally address is settled: a session it already holds, or one it spawned itself. There is no lookup, and there is not going to be one (thread 2).

Fan-out is ruled out, not deferred

A request runs in a session. That is where its items, its state and its continuity live, and nothing is processed without one — a design invariant, not an accident of the current code. The tempting order is to build fan-out first: publish to a topic and let any interested party pick it up. It cannot be built, because an unaddressed message has no session, so there is nothing for it to run in.

Delivering to every interested party would mean inventing a session per subscriber. That is a different feature — spawning work — wearing delivery's name. So fan-out is not a later version of mail. It is incoherent with the thing underneath it, and the design is smaller for saying so out loud.

A flow is already a mailroom

Actions are message types, and the flow routes each one to its workflow. That is what defineFlow({ actions }) has always been, and it is why so little has to be added: mail is a new kind of sender arriving at a door that already exists.

Actions are already API-driven — over REST, or programmatically against the flow instance — so the set of things that can put a message through that door is already open. Mail adds an internal sender to it. It does not add a second way in.

The machinery is small

The process executing the current request sends. The same process, conceptually on another thread, receives the message and handles it as a request on the addressed session. Where a queue adapter is configured — @flow-state-dev/bullmq is the one in the tree — delivery is a job, and that is what makes it survive a process restart. For an in-process host, delivery is attached to the process, not to the originating web request, so a sender that has already returned does not take its message with it.

Arbitration is not new either. The concurrency policy is “enforced once at the shared dispatch seam, so every transport … inherits the same behavior” — and mail inherits it with everything else. Mail either waits for the session to finish its in-flight requests (queue, FIFO on the key) or is handled as soon as it arrives (allow). One word on the flow. No new queueing mechanism.

Cron becomes a sender, not a transport of its own

The same seam answers a second thing the project has open. A schedule fires, sends mail to an addressed session, and the bus routes it exactly like any other message. Two concrete gaps stand between here and there, and both are visible in the tree today.

  • A scheduled tick has no session at all. The scheduled routes pass none, and the code says why in as many words: “scheduled envelopes have no session.” So there is no way to address a specific session from a cron. It also means a tick gets no arbitration under the default policy, whose "session" key “resolves to undefined (no arbitration) when the dispatch has no session”.
  • A dynamic schedule carries a user, not a session. The schedule row's schema is { cron, kind, input, timezone, onOverlap, description, enabled }, and the resolver parses (userId, collectionKey) and synthesizes principal: { userId }. The address cannot even be expressed.

The shape settled on is one optional session address on the schedule row, and it serves both cases at once. Absent means a new session per run — today's behaviour, preserved exactly. Present means each run is a new request on that session. proposed — the cron changes and the mail build are a separate effort, now filed as one epic with the cron half as its own issue, and this atlas states the constraint rather than the design. Section 11 has the order.

Two things to carry into that build

Self-addressed mail under queue is a deadlock shape, so refuse it. queue runs one request to completion before the next starts, so a sender that awaits its own mail never lets it start. Fire-and-forget is fine; awaiting is not — and the pairing should be rejected at definition or dispatch time, so the deadlock is unrepresentable rather than merely documented (thread 4).

Fan-in collapsing is anticipated, and additive. debounce is already reserved in the policy enum — it parses, and is rejected at definition time in v1 — and it is exactly what “three workstreams posted questions, wake the coordinator once” wants. The collapse case has a home without changing the shape.

The coordinator's job, and why it stays small

When the coordinator wakes it does one thing: read the open inbox rows, ask about them, take your reply, and route it. That is a classification problem over a set of size one to three. It is not a re-derivation of the epic, and it never needs to be, because everything that would justify a larger context is already in a collection somebody else can read.

Three workstreams asking at once is the ordinary case, not the edge case. The coordinator serializes. One inbox, one question at a time, and the answer routed back to the run that asked. That is deliberately the opposite of per-workstream question threads, where you would address any of three channels in any order — which is exactly the juggling of parallel Claude sessions this project exists to replace. Recreating it inside the tool would forfeit the point.

And the classify-and-route step is itself a flow made of blocks, not one large model turn. A small structured generator decides which open rows a reply answers; a router dispatches one steer per answered row. Both primitives ship today: intentClassifier and intentRouter live in @flow-state-dev/core, and router({ routes, execute }) is a first-class block kind.

The honest hard case. A reply that answers one row cleanly is easy. Three cases are not, and the design has to say what happens rather than hope:

  • Ambiguous across two rows. Two open rows about the same file, one reply that fits both. The classifier returns two candidates at similar confidence; below a threshold the coordinator does not guess — it asks back, naming both rows. Guessing routes a decision into the wrong worktree, which is expensive and silent.
  • Answers one row and opens a new topic. "Use the second option — and while you're there, drop the retry wrapper." Only the answer is classifier output — { rowId, answer, conf } has no field for anything else. It settles its row and rides the steer back. The new topic is a request aimed at the coordinator, the board's single writer (thread 5), and this path has no route that takes it there. proposed So the reply half-lands: the answer arrives, the topic does not.
  • Answers nothing. A question back at the coordinator. It answers from the collections if it can, and otherwise opens nothing and settles nothing — the rows stay open, which is the correct outcome and the one an eager classifier gets wrong.

Keying on ${topic}/${phase} gives each its own, and costs no config change: runs/** is a deep wildcard, so runs/ISSUE-B/implement already matches, and a list under ISSUE-B still gathers that issue's runs.

sharedToWorkstream is why this is the right primitive rather than a near-enough one. Its contract: “Give this session-scoped collection ONE identity across the whole session lineage. A session and the background child sessions it spawns resolve the collection against the lineage root, so they read and write one instance set.” The coordinator's session and every workstream it spawns therefore address one set of rows without the index going user-wide — and the set dies with the lineage, which is coherent, because the SDK sessions it points at die then too. Session scope only: defineResourceCollection rejects the flag at user or org scope, and rejects any scope that is not "session", "user" or "org".

One row per run — not one row holding an array of runs. N concurrent workstreams read-modify-writing a single row lose each other's writes, and that contention is exactly what a collection removes. "All sessions" is a list(), not an array you rewrite. And if the index should later outlive the session lineage — a handle you want on Monday for a run that started Friday — the same collection moves to scope: "user" and drops the flag. That is a scope change, not a redesign.

The write side needs nothing from the agent block. Its detached guard is on persistence alone, and the comment sitting on it draws the line explicitly: “Skipped wholesale when conversation state is off — a value written under an undeclared key is a silent corruption. The handle is still RETURNED; that is this block's output, not persisted state.” The returned SdkAgentHandle carries sessionId whether or not the block is detached. The manager takes it off the output and writes it onto the task.

What a phase actually is

The question worth asking before writing three adapters: what about a phase requires a custom block sequence, rather than a prompt and a pass-off to the harness? Answered honestly, the design gets smaller.

Take each phase and separate what is genuinely specific to it from what is the same harness management wearing a different name.

spec The prompt (what a spec must contain). The done-condition: a spec exists on its branch and is mirrored. The readable set: the epic and the issue, not the diff. And one structural difference — a spec's completion is followed by a human gate, so its task has a downstream approval dependency the other phases do not. Provisioning. Starting the run. Letting the turn finish. Reading the handle. Deciding settle / retry / ask. Posting to the inbox. Resuming on a steer. Settling the row.
implement The prompt (the spec, plus the previous attempt's feedback). The done-condition: a PR exists and its checks are green. The readable set: the issue and its approved spec. All of the above, unchanged.
review The prompt (what a review checks). The done-condition: a verdict is recorded and the round count is under budget. The readable set: the issue, the spec, the diff. The verdict

Three phases differ in three values, not in three block sequences. A prompt builder, a done-condition predicate, and a list of readable collections. Everything else — the workspace, the turn loop, the stall and question handling, the retry, the settle — is one harness manager every phase shares. So this is one block of substance and three small records, not three adapters. Adding a fourth phase costs a record.

Which is why the name matters. "Phase adapter" implies three things to write and three places for behaviour to drift. Harness manager plus phase logic says where the work is and where it isn't.

A phase needs a recipe: what a spec must contain, what a review checks, what counts as done. Those exist today as the development skills — markdown files read by the Claude Code harness. They do not need to become framework skills. The meta-harness and the harness want different instructions anyway: one is told how to run a process, the other how to write code. If a file ever has to live in both places, a symlink solves it. A note, not a blocker.

Sessions know what the others are working on

A planner reasoning alone plans work that collides with a sibling's. So a planning or architect role gets the other epics in flight in its generator context: what each one is doing, how far along it is, when it last moved. Two things follow. It stops proposing work another session already owns — and, better, it can decide to ask before it commits, raising the assumption with the operator through its own session while the plan is still cheap to change. Deconfliction is the floor. Coordination is the point. These rows complement the architect's org-scoped memory (section 1) rather than substituting for it: resources hold what has been decided across epics, this set holds what is moving right now.

This is a collection, not a framework session registry, and that call is the interesting part. A registry answers “session abc123 is alive”. What a planner needs is “this epic is refactoring the store adapters, three issues open, last moved an hour ago”. Each coordinator writes its own row as it works; any role reads the set through a context formatter over a list(). Four reasons it belongs here rather than in the engine:

  • It carries meaning a liveness registry cannot. Engine state is thin on purpose; the thing a planner is asking is a domain question.
  • It is this document's own principle applied again — state in a resource, not in context. The coordinator's memory already lives in rows. So does its picture of its peers.
  • It reaches a generator through the context slot, which is how every other context in this system is delivered. No new delivery path to invent.
  • It survives session death. A registry loses the epic when the process goes. A row persists, and can say “stalled” instead of vanishing.

So the framework piece is close to nothing: coordinators write their own rows, and reading them is a list() a formatter renders. proposed What is left over usually gets filed as one question — staleness — and it is really two. Does this session exist is cheap, and safe to answer: a session id either names a record or it does not, and saying so leaks nothing a peer's own row was not already publishing. Is this request still live is a different question, and the engine guards it deliberately.

The design sidesteps both, which is why neither has to be built. A sender declares that a message expects a response and gives up on its own clock. That needs no visibility into the other session at all: the sender knows what it asked and when, and a timeout is a fact it already owns. It is the smallest thing that works, and it covers a case a probe cannot even describe — it is not clear what should make a session stale in the first place, since a request can hang for an hour inside a session that is perfectly present. A sender-side timeout answers the question the sender actually has, which is should I still be waiting.

The caveat, verbatim — and it is a trap

livenessOf?(requestIds: readonly string[]): Promise<LivenessAnswers>, where LivenessAnswers = Readonly<Record<string, boolean>>. Its own documentation: “false means ‘no live registration was found’, never ‘definitely dead.’” A request that completed, one never registered, and one whose registration was lost are indistinguishable by construction, because terminal requests are deregistered. “Treat false as permission to stop waiting — never as proof the work did not happen.”

So it is the right instrument for “should I still be waiting on this” and the wrong one for “did that work happen”. Two limits bind this use of it in particular. The verb is optional and absent when the liveness gate refuses (section 9). And identity filters before the answer is built — user, tenant, flow kind, then descendant-session — with the module stating the consequence in as many words: “an id outside the caller's lineage or under a different principal comes back indistinguishable from an unknown id — there is no existence oracle here.” A peer coordinator's session is outside that lineage, since the walk only ever climbs to the caller's own. Which is the argument for the timeout again. The sender's own clock is the answer; the lookup corroborates it — and only for one's own descendants.

The rows are intelligence, not an address book. A planner reads them to see what a peer is holding and how far along it is. Nothing in the set is a way to reach that peer. A sender addresses a session it already holds, or one it spawned itself (thread 2).

Memory — deferred on purpose, not ruled out

We talk to our agent, which fans out to Claude Code most of the time. So it is genuinely unclear whether Conductor needs memory of its own or can let the harness use its. We expect to hold memory eventually, bespoke and tuned to this workflow, not general conversation retention. Note that the bounded-coordinator design lowers the pressure for it: a coordinator that reasons over open rows has less to remember by construction.

Many runs, one session

One board, several harness runs at once, and no registry of them anywhere. Concurrency is addressed by topic and read back from durable rows.

Each concurrent run is identified by a derived child session id, hashed from tenant, principal, parent session, topic, and key. topic comes from task.metadata.topic, falling back to taskId. The worker coordinate is {kind:"assignee",name} | {kind:"uniform"} | {kind:"floor"}, and the spawn seed is workstreamRoutingSeed({ boardId, coordinate, topic, topicFallback: claim.taskId }). The child enters with source: "workstream" and resolves flow.workstream, which is terminal — flow.actions is unreachable from inside a workstream. That terminality is what the call-site rule of section 1 rests on. It does not by itself stop a dispatched role writing to the board, which is reachable as a capability from anywhere — keeping one writer is a choice the design makes, not one the framework makes for it (thread 5).

Topic is the discriminator, and section 6 leans on it. Deriving identity from the topic is exactly what makes a steer routable: the coordinator reads the topic off the inbox row, and the same seed lands in the same child session. It also means two implement runs on one issue that must proceed concurrently need different topics, and two runs that should share a session need the same one. The design has to say which, per phase.

Open threads

Fourteen threads this design cannot close on its own. Five now have answers, settled by the work section 11 puts on the roadmap; they are kept here with their reasoning rather than deleted, because a decision without its reasoning cannot be re-checked. The other nine are still live.

1 · Nothing has driven one real issue end to end through this seam

Every diagram here routes through the harness manager. The manager ships in labs/conductor. The parts it joins also exist: the board claims and routes, claudeCodeAgent runs, startDetached spawns, and the runner's fenced recorders close the row. What is still unproven is driving one real issue end to end on a live Claude child — the join is where the surprises live, particularly around a run that asks something on its first turn.

Answered — yes, and it is filed as an epic of its own: the manager, then one real issue driven end to end, question and all, on throwaway provisioning (section 11). What is still outstanding is the objective gate on it, not the approach.
2 · Mail, and the cron changes that ride on it

The cold path has a named answer: a workstream sends mail to the coordinator’s session, and the flow handles it as a request like any other input (section 6). The build is no longer open — it is an approved epic with five issues under it, and this document still deliberately does not specify it. Its shape is what section 6 argued for: an address on the envelope, a send verb for a running request, and per-adapter delivery, plus the cron half, a schedule that can name a session so a tick stops being a session-less dispatch and becomes a sender like everything else. The sibling-spawn verb is scoped with that layer rather than after it — same addressing, same per-adapter delivery. One addition sits on the board rather than the bus, it never depended on the send verb, and it has already shipped: the parked-task exit mode section 6 records, filed under the honest-task-substrate epic rather than inside Relay. The watch stays in Relay — a keyed row per (watcher, task) plus a best-effort send inside the settle, which borrows the send verb and needs nothing else.

The question the existing seam raises deliberately has an answer too, and it is a subtraction. RequestHost never takes a session id as a parameter — a caller supplies only a seed, and identity is closed over — precisely so nobody can name a session they do not own. What a sender may legally address is settled by ruling discovery out of the layer entirely: a sender addresses a session it already holds, or one it spawned itself. There is no lookup, and there is not going to be one.

Answered — the Proof is relay plus a parked task row, and it waits for both. The earlier plan was to start on the hot path, hold the question in awaiting_review while the operator answered, and adopt relay afterwards. That is not what gets built. A worker slot held open for as long as an answer takes is the thing section 6 says not to depend on, and starting on it is how a stopgap quietly becomes the design. The first real run parks its task, ends its request, and is woken by a relayed message.
3 · What this is called

This document says mail, and that name is not settled. The competing shape is not a synonym, it is a different decomposition. Message is already uniform in this system — a user message, a session message — so instead of standing up a parallel noun, extend messages with a sender and a recipient. Cron then falls out as a scheduled message rather than a second concept: one noun, several origins.

If the flow-config group still wants a qualifier, relay is the accurate one — it names routing, which is what this does, rather than storage. exchange is rejected: AMQP-loaded, and an exchange is a fan-out primitive, the one thing this design rules out. intake is rejected too — it implies inbound-only, and this is bidirectional. Mail's virtue is that it reads as internal; its risk is that a mailbox implies polling, which is exactly the model the design rejects.

Answered — relay. It is the name the filed epic carries, so it is what the config surface inherits and what every later doc repeats. This document keeps saying mail in the prose above because renaming it here would cost more than it clarifies; read the two as the same thing.
4 · How many send modes

Fire-and-forget and wait-for-response are genuinely different intents, and both are wanted: a workstream posting a question does not wait, a planner checking an assumption against a neighbouring epic does. A third mode, delivery receipt, is questionable. It answers did it arrive, which only matters if you cannot otherwise tell — and a durable row already tells you.

The related design move is not open, and belongs on the same page as the modes. Self-addressed plus wait-for-response should be refused at definition or dispatch time. Under queue a sender that awaits its own message never lets it start, so the deadlock should be unrepresentable rather than merely documented.

Answered — two modes in the spec, one of them this cycle. The floor is addressing plus fire-and-forget; wait-for-response stays specified and lands in a later PR, and nothing on the Proof's path needs it, because a workstream posting a question does not wait for a reply (section 11). The refusal came with the design and is wider than the deadlock: a blocking send is rejected outright whenever the effective dispatcher cannot serve it, which is what made the cross-worker wake channel conditional — and that condition has since resolved to later, with the mode it served. A receipt is still out, and what would change that is unchanged: a delivery path where a message can be accepted and then silently dropped.
5 · May some workstreams manage the board directly?

Section 6 states the answer the design runs on: a workstream requests a plan change, and the top session decides. That keeps the board single-writer, which is what makes it reasonable about — one author per transition, and one place to look when a task is in a state nobody expects.

The doubt is real, though. Some workstreams sit closer to the plan than the coordinator does: a review run that finds an issue needs splitting knows more about the split than anyone upstream, and routing it through a request adds a hop and a translation. A narrow write grant — this worker may add sub-tasks under its own issue, and nothing else — is a coherent middle rather than a fudge.

Unresolved. The argument for the stated answer is that single-writer is a property you lose all at once and cannot cheaply get back, while the request path costs a hop rather than a capability. What would change it: a plan-change round trip measurably slower than the work it gates.
6 · A detached agent has no way to be handed a resume id

The design's one real dependency on framework code, and it is small. claudeCodeAgent consults its session provider only on the non-detached path, so nothing today carries a prior sdkSessionId into a detached run. Until something does, a steer starts the coding agent from nothing: the workstream keeps its item history and the model loses its own. The write half already works — the handle is returned regardless of detached — so what is missing is an input and a conditional resume, not a change to what detached means.

Answered — resume, not restart. Restarting is correct and expensive: a re-stated prompt pays again for a diff the agent had already read, and a Proof whose second turn does that is not proving the loop it claims to. So the input is on the Proof's critical path rather than beside it, and shipping the Proof without it is off the table. Two things follow that are not settled by this answer: the shape of the typed handle field on the task (section 6), and what the resume work lands as — thread 14.
7 · A reply the classifier cannot place

The coordinator's whole economy rests on classification being reliable over a small set. Two open rows about the same file and one reply that fits both is not a contrived case — it is Tuesday. The design says ask back below a confidence threshold, which is safe and mildly annoying. It does not yet say what the threshold is, or whether repeated ask-backs are worse than an occasional misroute.

Does a misrouted answer cost more than a re-ask? If a steer into the wrong worktree is cheap to undo, bias toward guessing; if it silently changes the wrong file, bias toward asking. This is a product call about tone as much as correctness.
8 · Steer delivery is bounded, not instant

Under queue, a steer lands at the next turn boundary. A long turn means a long wait, and the only true interrupt is the mid-turn abort — there is no "stop what you are doing and read this" that preserves the run. That is probably right, since interrupting mid-tool-call is how state gets corrupted, but it is a constraint the UX has to be honest about rather than hide.

Do we show that a steer is queued behind an in-flight turn, or let it look instant and absorb the delay? Showing it is more honest and slightly noisier.
9 · Durability over a multi-day run

The success criterion is literally "multi-day without degrading". A multi-day epic is the longest-running, most failure-exposed workload we have, and the board's load-bearing areas are lease, retry and durable resume: a stranded lease can hang a drain for hours, a reclaim can reset every in-flight task, a dead detached child can look stalled for a full lease, and a blind retry re-runs unchanged work. Resting the question channel on the cold path removes one of these — no worker slot and no lease is held across a human's answer, because the workstream's request ends and its task waits as a durable row — and leaves the rest untouched.

Harden the board first, or find these on the first real epic? These are not adjacent bugs — they are the criterion's failure modes, and the third clause fails on exactly this class.
10 · Nothing measures the criterion

"Better than juggling Claude mega sessions" and "multi-day epics without degrading" are real, checkable claims. There is no baseline for the mega-session route, no working definition of degradation, and no instrument for either. Nor is there a goal check that runs a development process end to end — every green check today certifies a part.

What is the smallest honest measurement? One epic run both ways, recording wall-clock, gate count, rework rounds, questions asked, and a short operator note, would beat nothing — and it is the only proof this design currently has a route to.
11 · Which layer of workforce do Conductor’s roles sit on?

A package in this repo already materializes a role as a block. agentBlock(agent) returns a BlockDefinition — documented as “invoke an Agent as a standalone block in any flow” — and it sits alongside defineAgent, definePersona, createAgentRegistry, materializeAgent and createWorkforceCapability, with a per-agent tool catalog and capability catalog behind each one. That is close to exactly the coordinator / architect / manager shape of section 1.

But @flow-state-dev/workforce is not a settled package. It was intended as a file-conventions system — an opinionated wrapper that takes primitives already sitting underneath it and packages them into something easier to work with. It is not a finished thing to adopt wholesale. So the fork is not reuse it or build our own. It is which layer Conductor actually needs: the wrapper, or the primitives beneath it.

The layering is visible in the source, which is what makes the question answerable rather than a matter of taste. agentBlock is a thin wrapper over materializeAgent; materializeAgent builds on workerInputSchema, buildUserMessage and the task-tools capability, all of which live in @flow-state-dev/orchestration, and it is written to satisfy MaterializeAgentFn so it can be injected rather than depended on. Hence an outcome worth naming now: if the only piece Conductor ever uses is materialize a role as a block, that piece may belong in @flow-state-dev/orchestration rather than Conductor taking a dependency on a wrapper for one function.

Four things checked since, and they point one way. The persona machinery is separable at essentially one line — materialize-agent.ts's call to resolveAgentPersona — and its types, PersonaSource and PersonaInlineConfig, already live in @flow-state-dev/core rather than in workforce. define-persona.ts and resolve-persona.ts import nothing from workforce at all. Orchestration is already the dependency direction: workforce imports workerInputSchema, buildUserMessage and taskTools from orchestration, so moving the useful part down creates no cycle. And createAgentRegistry is fifteen lines around a Map, needed not by agentBlock but only by the agent-ref string-indirection path in orchestration's own delegation — which is, tellingly, the only part of workforce anything in the tree actually uses.

Still unresolved, and the leaning has moved. It was toward building on workforce, on the argument that “different roles within a team” is precisely what workforce was reaching for and a second materializer would be duplicate surface. The evidence now points instead at moving the agent-as-block piece down into orchestration, rather than taking a dependency on a wrapper for one function. Neither is locked.

Not a decision to make now. It is one to make with the first real role block in front of us, because the layer only becomes obvious once something needs it. What to confirm then is the cheap outcome the evidence now favours — one function moving down into orchestration, and neither package adopted at all.
12 · Does the manager decide to re-plan, or only say that re-planning is needed?

The manager reads the board and pushes — something is stalled, a workstream is not progressing, the plan no longer fits what the work turned out to be. What it may then do is not settled. It could adjust the plan itself, inside the approved objective, and tell you afterwards. Or it could surface that re-planning is needed and let the coordinator put the question to you.

That is the difference between a manager and a reporter, and it is a product call rather than an implementation detail: it sets how much autonomy the system takes on your behalf while you are not watching.

Leaning: surface rather than decide, until the manager has a track record you can read. Deciding is the better product if it is right most of the time and the worse one if a bad re-plan surfaces a day later — and there is no evidence yet about which. What would change it: a run of epics where the re-plans it proposes are approved unchanged.
13 · A workstream shares its parent’s whole flow

There is no separate Workstream flow. A detached child runs the parent’s own FlowInstance, under the parent’s flow.kind, and the kind is not a construction input at all — packages/engine/src/context/detached-start-operation.ts says it “arrives per call, from the seam, as the parent request’s own kind. A child always belongs to its parent’s flow.” The binding is one line, flowKind: flow.kind in packages/engine/src/context/create-request-host.ts. The identifier WorkstreamFlow appears exactly once in the repository, inside a comment, and names nothing; what is shared is flow.workstream, one ActionCore assembled onto that same flow beside flow.actions (packages/core/src/flow/workstream-core.ts) — the comment is what makes people picture a second flow object.

So everything declared on the flow definition is shared by construction: actions, the resources map, session.stateSchema, webhooks?, schedules?. If the Relay spec’s placement holds, relay? joins them — spec/_epics/relay.md declares it as “a relay? group, a sibling of webhooks? / schedules?”, and both of those are per-flow fields on FlowDefinition (packages/core/src/types/flow.ts).

Two doors lead into a workstream, and only one is narrowed. The seam door, a framework-stamped detached dispatch, routes terminally to flow.workstream and can never reach flow.actions (packages/engine/src/execution/resolve-action-core.ts). The caller door is not. A child session is an ordinary session of the same flowKind owned by the same principal, and no route-level guard excludes it, so the parent’s full action surface is present inside the workstream to anything that can address that session. The narrow inside-world surface is a routing choice, not a boundary.

The shared-flow model already costs something, and the code says so out loud. packages/orchestration/src/task-board/detached.ts refuses a detached worker whose descendants declare sessionStateSchema, because “detached workers share one Workstream flow, where two routes choosing the same key with different shapes corrupt each other with no error.” The refusal is defensible; its stated reason names the wrong mechanism. Two detached workers land in two different child sessions with two different state blobs — board, coordinate and topic must all three match before two tasks share a workstream — so there is no shared cell to corrupt. What they share is the flow-level key namespace: one flow.session.stateSchema for the parent and every descendant, with a block’s sessionStateSchema a pure typing overlay that gets no runtime parse. A comment to correct, not a behaviour to fix.

The fork is narrower than the fact makes it look. Per-role divergence costs nothing and is already the shape: “all workstreams get surface X, all top-level sessions get surface Y” fits inside one flat relay? group today, which is the asymmetric door the Relay spec already draws: a sibling resolves flow.actions because it is a caller, a workstream gets “a narrow, author-declared inside-world surface” and never reaches flow.actions at all. Per-instance divergence is what nothing reserves room for: this workstream addressable at A and that one at B, or a child narrower than its parent. That needs the declared surface to gain a keyed or resolver form rather than a flat group, plus a resolution step that does not exist. It needs almost no new persisted rows — SessionRecord already carries parentSessionId, topic and coordinate alongside flowKind (packages/engine/src/stores/types.ts) — but the recipient lookup reads only flowKind, and nothing else reads that row for relay resolution.

Leaning: per-role is enough, so do not build the keyed form. Conductor’s workstreams are all one kind of thing, a phase run, and the differences it wants per phase (askPolicy, model, gate) already live in the phase table the manager reads at dispatch, not in the relay door. Say it rather than leave it silent, though: Relay’s first issue is where the seam gets reserved or consciously closed, and its spec is silent on parent-versus-child divergence, which closes it by default. A seam closed by decision and one closed by silence cost the same today and very different amounts later. What being wrong costs: widening a flat relay? group to a keyed or resolver form, pre-1.0, no persisted-row migration implied. Cheap, which is what makes the restrained call a default rather than a gamble.
14 · Does the resume POC land as a path, or as a harness?

The Proof needs resume rather than restart (thread 6), so the work that proves it sits on the critical path rather than beside it. What that work leaves behind is not decided. It could land as a path the workstreams epic can call — a real seam, carried forward, at the cost of having to get its shape right before anything has resumed once. Or it could land as a throwaway harness that establishes the behaviour and is then deleted, with the seam built afterwards against a known answer.

Not colour to bury at the end of a section. The two produce different plans: one puts the seam's design inside the Proof, the other puts it after — and the typed handle field on the task (section 6) is undesigned either way. It belongs to the parent resume issue, which is where the shape of the resume input is being settled in any case.

Which does the Proof need — a callable path, or a proof and a delete? Both ways of being wrong are the expensive kind. A throwaway that quietly becomes the path is the one this document keeps naming; a permanent seam designed before anything has resumed once is the other. Answered inside the resume issue it costs a paragraph. Answered after the Proof ships it costs a rewrite.
The one-sentence version

Conductor is a meta-harness for our own SDLC, built on a substrate that already has the flow, the board, the detached runs and the parked task row — and its bet is that the session you talk to stays small, because the open questions live in an inbox rather than in its head.
