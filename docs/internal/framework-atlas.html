01

The system map

FSD is one framework with three distinct jobs, and almost every architectural question comes down to which of the three a piece of code belongs to.

Authoring is isomorphic: contracts and core give you the block primitives and the type contracts, and the composition libraries stack on top of them without ever touching a runtime. Running is server-only: engine owns execution, state, persistence, and the wire. Consuming is client-side: client speaks the wire, react wraps client, and nothing below them knows either exists.

The handoff between authoring and running is a flow definition. The handoff between running and consuming is JSON plus an SSE stream. Those two seams are the reason the boundary rules in §06 can be enforced at all.

What the framework is trying to be

From docs/philosophy.md: FSD is infrastructure, not an application. It absorbs the plumbing every AI feature rebuilds — call a model, stream the response, hold state, retry, render, resume — as composable primitives, and it deliberately refuses to absorb any one app's domain knowledge or any one vendor's specifics.

Two failure modes are ranked, and the ranking drives most of the architecture:

  1. Incoherence — patterns that disagree with each other. Primary, because no single-diff review catches it.
  2. Bloat — surface that never earned its place. A close second, and the thing that becomes incoherence if left alone.
Why this matters for a refactor

The philosophy already says the codebase carries known bloat and incoherence that accreted before the tenets were written down. This atlas is most useful read as a map of where that surface actually sits — §26 collects the specific tensions worth deciding on.

02

What decides what runs next

Every workflow system answers one question: who decides what happens next, and when do they decide it? FSD refuses to pick a single answer. It ships four, on one substrate, and lets them nest inside each other.

This is the axis the whole framework is organized around, and it is the thing to hold in mind before reading any of the per-system sections below. A sequencer is not a lesser task board and a task board is not a worse sequencer — they sit at different points on a dial that trades predictability for adaptability, and the right answer depends on how much you know about the work before it starts.

They nest, which is the actual point

Because all four terminate in BlockDefinition, the modes compose without adapters:

  • A sequencer step can be a task board — parallelTasks, supervisor, and planAndExecute are exactly that.
  • A board worker can be a sequencer, so one node of a dynamic graph can be a fixed pipeline.
  • A generator holding a skill can command its own private board — the skill plans tasks, runBoard drains them.
  • A board task can spawn detached, re-entering later through that same board's runner in a child session.

So the practical design move is not "which mode is this application?" but "at which point in this pipeline does the shape stop being knowable?" Everything before that point should be a sequencer. Everything after it needs a board or a skill. Most real systems put the boundary somewhere in the middle, and the framework's job is to make that seam invisible.

The cost that is easy to miss

Determinism is not only about test flakiness — it is about resume. A sequencer resumes by skipping committed steps. A router resumes by re-validating a recorded decision, and throws rather than silently branching elsewhere. A board resumes off its durable ledger. A model-written plan has no recorded shape to resume into: re-running re-plans. That is the real price of the right-hand end of the dial, and it is why the board sits between the two — it gives you a dynamic graph that is still durable.

03

The shared substrate

The four modes are interchangeable because they stand on identical ground. A board worker, a sequencer step, and a task a model just invented all receive the same ctx and reach the same six systems.

Those six systems are orthogonal because each answers a different question. That is the decomposition the framework is actually selling — most AI stacks tangle all six into one "agent" object, and then cannot vary any of them independently.

Why the orthogonality earns its keep

Because the six vary independently, you can change one without touching the rest. Make a chat pipeline concurrent by swapping a forEach for a board — the scopes, the resources, and the item routing are untouched. Hide a sub-agent's chatter from the user while keeping it in the parent's context — that is one itemVisibility field, not a rearchitecture. Move work off the request without rewriting it — startDetached, and the same block runs in a child session.

The two places the axes genuinely meet are worth knowing, because they are where the system gets its power:

  • Resources are how concurrent actors share. The TaskCollection is resource-backed, so the board's ledger is not a special store — it is the ordinary resource system under a task-shaped schema. That is why a board gets CAS, change events, client projection, and reactive blocks without asking for any of them.
  • The board and the workstream meet at the runner. A detached dispatch carries a boardId, never a worker — it re-enters through the declaring board's runner, which re-reads the claimed row, re-verifies the claim, and only then routes to the assignee the durable row names. There is no path from a detached dispatch to a bare worker block, which is what makes "a task that outlives the turn" safe rather than a forgeable entry point.

04

Workforce: the layer that isn't built

Workforce is meant to be the top of the stack — teams of named agents that work together, powered by every system beneath them. Today it is 397 lines that define what one agent is, and nothing that defines how a set of them relate.

What exists

The Agent contract itself lives in core (so orchestration can type an injected materializeAgent without depending on workforce). An agent is a name, a description, a persona, a model, a tool allow-list, capabilities, and item visibility. materializeAgent turns that into a generator — resolving the persona into a system prompt, pulling tools and capabilities from catalogs, and binding taskTools to the active drain board when the agent is being materialized as a board worker.

definePersona is the piece worth noticing: it is sugar over defineResource / defineResourceCollection with a content template, which means an agent's identity is a resource — editable at runtime, renderable from state, and addressable by path. That is the same move skills make with Markdown, applied to who an agent is rather than what it should do.

Where teams actually happen today

Not in workforce. The one place a set of agents collaborates is the skills delegation surface: a skill declaring agents: installs a private task board, materializes each declared agent as a board worker, seats every allowed tool in the same assignee namespace as those agents, and hands the executive generator the eight taskTools plus runBoard. The model then plans work as tasks with an assignee and deps, and drains the whole graph in one call.

That is a real multi-agent team, and it is complete. But it is a team inside one skill — the roster is that skill's frontmatter, the board is that generator's own state, and none of it outlives the turn or is addressable from anywhere else.

What "a team" would mean, given what already exists

Most of the machinery is there. An agent already has an editable identity, a model, tools, capabilities, and a place to do work. What a team layer would add is relationships and a lifetime:

  • A roster that is a thing, not a map. createAgentRegistry is a flat name-to-agent lookup with a duplicate check. A team has structure — who may assign to whom, who reviews whom, who is the executive. Today that structure exists only implicitly, as whatever the executive generator happens to plan.
  • A workspace scoped to the team, not to a turn. The pieces exist (sharedToWorkstream, resource collections, the lineage root) but nothing binds them to a roster. A standing team wants a durable board its members return to, not a board that lives inside one generator's state.
  • Composition of the two unwired fields. usesSkills would let a member carry its own playbooks rather than inheriting the coordinator's; contextMode: "fork" would give a member its own context window instead of composing inline. Both are the difference between "a prompt with a name" and "a participant".
The decision this stack is waiting on

Everything below workforce got built because a running system needed it. Workforce is the one layer specified top-down — the Agent type carries two fields nothing resolves, and its capability factory returns a name and a TODO. That is the shape tenet 3 warns about: a contract taken on before there was a caller.

The generous reading is that it is a placeholder held deliberately while the layers underneath matured — and they did mature; seven of the ten needs above are now met by systems that did not exist when Agent was first written.

Is a team a new primitive, or is it a composition of a roster resource + a durable board + per-member skills? If it is the second, workforce may not need to grow at all — it may need to shrink to defineAgent + materializeAgent and let the team be assembled from parts that already work.

06

The locked boundaries

Five dependency rules are declared canonical and unchangeable without architecture review. They are what makes the three-job split from §01 real rather than aspirational.

  • contracts imports no workspace package and declares no dependencies. core re-exports every symbol from its original path, so consumers are unaffected by where a type lives.
  • engine never depends on react or client.
  • client never depends on engine or react.
  • react has no transport logic — it wraps client.
  • cli uses engine + testing, never react or client.

Browser code value-imports the canonical helpers from contracts rather than hand-mirroring them; core stays type-only for those packages. That is the mechanism by which contracts earns its existence — without it, the item taxonomy would be duplicated on both sides of the wire.

07

Four block kinds

The whole framework rests on this: every piece of logic is exactly one of four kinds, and all four share one execution contract. That uniformity is what makes composition free.

handler generator sequencer router

What the runtime does at the seam

Blocks never call block.config.execute directly. Everything goes through executeBlock, and that is where the framework inserts what a block author would otherwise hand-roll:

Validation
both directions

Input and output schemas checked at the boundary; a sequencer's declared output schema is a runtime chokepoint, not just a type.

Retry & rescue
policy, not code

Retry policies apply per block; rescue handlers catch by error type at the sequencer or the block level.

Provenance
block_trace

Every block emits a lifecycle trace item carrying a BlockValue — ref, inline, or structure — so the execution tree is reconstructable without duplicating payloads.

Declaration bubbling
upward merge

State schemas and resource declarations made on a leaf block bubble up through sequencers to the flow. A counter block declares only its counter.

Lifecycle hooks
past tense, observational

onStarted, onCompleted, onErrored, onFinished. Deliberately observational — there is no block middleware, by design.

Connections
wiring, not adapters

connectInput / connectOutput reshape a block's edges at the call site, so a block stays reusable without a wrapper.

Generator anatomy

The generator is the one leaf that owns a control loop, which makes it the most configuration-dense primitive in the framework. Four prompt slots resolve in order — prompt, context, history, user — each accepting a string, object, array, or async function. Object-form context aggregates by XML tag name, so a capability and the block itself can both contribute to <documents> and land in one section rather than two.

Prompts can also be authored as Markdown files: YAML frontmatter over a LiquidJS body split into <system>, <user>, and <context> sections, rendered against input, ctx, and the post-resolution config, with sibling .md files auto-registered as partials.

Loop ownership depends on the model. A step-capable model (the built-in AI SDK adapter is one) hands the multi-step loop to FSD: one provider call per step, framework tools executed by the framework — concurrently within a step, through its own executor, cache, retry, and tool_output path. Models without step methods keep the older SDK-driven generate({ maxSteps }) path. That ownership is the substrate for in-loop suspension.

  • tap(block)side effect, payload unchanged
  • tapIf(cond, block)conditional side effect
  • exitIf(cond)early exit from the chain
  • throwIf(cond, error)invariant check
  • rescue(handlers)recover by error type

Two properties are worth holding onto when reasoning about a redesign. First, side chains are non-aborting by default — a failure there does not stop the main chain unless waitForSideChain({ failOnError: true }) promotes it. Second, conditional step variants are preferred over wrapper sequencers (BP-036): .sideChainIf / .tapIf / .stepIf with an inline connector, rather than wrapping a block in a sequencer whose only job is the condition.

Schema propagation runs both at build time (.validate(), with known limitations) and as a runtime chokepoint. Sequencers also carry their own state schema, which bubbles into the flow's session schema alongside the state schemas that leaf blocks declare.

Flows and actions

A flow is the unit of deployment: it binds blocks to entry points, declares the state scopes they may touch, and decides what any of it is allowed to show a client.

The concept that unifies the entry points is ActionCore — the executable unit plus its execution policy, independent of how it is addressed or authorized. Two address forms build on it, and the runtime records both identically.

State and scopes

Four nested scopes, one operation set, and a hard structural rule: raw state never reaches a client.

What sits on top of the scope model

CAS everywhere
concurrency

Every write is compare-and-swap guarded, with a scope lock, a serialized write queue, and delta-verb routing so concurrent increments don't clobber each other.

Session journal & metadata
session scope

Items, messages, a journal, and mutable metadata (title, description, tags) — with an auto title generator utility for the common case.

Block-level state
locality

A block declares a partial schema for just its own fields. Schemas bubble up through sequencers to the flow, so nothing needs a god-object state type.

Tenant isolation
multi-tenant

Store-key isolation by (scopeId, flowKind, ref) or (scopeId, ref), with a binding check for key ambiguity. Cross-flow state is shared by default, isolated on opt-in.

Transient slots
non-persisted

State fields that live for the request and never hit the store — used for the status slot and other ephemeral signals.

Workstream scope
detached children

A child session that outlives its request. Each scope re-resolves inside it, and resources can be shared to the lineage explicitly.

Resources

Where state is the scope's own record, a resource is a named, typed container with a policy attached — who may read it, who may write it, whether a model may touch it, and how it reaches a client.

A resource carries an intrinsic scope (session, user, or org) and lives in one flat map on a flow, a block, or a capability. The accessor key is a typed read handle for ctx.resources.<name>; persistence is keyed by ref identity, so two blocks declaring the same DefinedResource under different accessor names share one storage slot.

Resource declarations behave like state schemas: a leaf block declares what it needs, sequencers merge upward, and the flow collects the union automatically. Capabilities contribute into the same merge. This is the mechanism that makes uses: [cap] replace manual plumbing — and it is also the mechanism that makes a same-accessor / different-ref collision a build-time error rather than a runtime surprise.

Execution and errors

A request is async by design: the POST returns 202 immediately, execution runs in the background, and the client watches it over SSE — then refetches the snapshot for the authoritative answer.

Failure handling, in layers

Retry policy
per block

Declared, not coded. Applies at the executeBlock seam so every kind gets it identically.

Rescue boundaries
by error type

Both a sequencer-level .rescue(handlers) and a block-level block.rescue. A rescue registry exposes ctx.wasRescued downstream.

Generator repair
schema failures

A structured-output miss gets a repair pass before it becomes a terminal error.

Side-chain isolation
non-aborting

Queued work runs off the main chain with its own signal lifecycle; it only becomes terminal if you ask for it.

Suspend and resume
durable actions

ctx.suspend() writes a suspension item with an allow list; the resume route gates inbound actions against it and 409s otherwise.

Crash recovery
replay

Durable frames in the checkpoint store let a re-entry skip committed work; a continuation item marks the seam between the prior log and the live re-run.

Error capture sink
observability

Provider errors are classified and normalized; a capture sink collects them without every call site handling them.

Token budgets
cost

tokenBudget on an ActionCore: maxTotalTokens, warnAt, onExceeded. Enforced by the runtime.

A deliberate absence

There is no block middleware, and docs/architecture/internal-execution-seams.md exists specifically to record why. Lifecycle hooks are observational and past-tense; the interception points are internal seams the framework owns, not an extension surface. Any redesign that reaches for "just add a middleware chain" is arguing with a decision that has already been made once.

Capabilities

A capability is a portable bundle — resources, state schemas, context, tools, helper functions — installed with one uses: [cap] entry instead of spreading four config slots by hand. It is the framework's answer to "how do I ship a feature that isn't a block?"

Capabilities shipped in the framework

Two authoring conventions are established. Configurable capabilities export a factory — createXCapability(options) → DefinedCapability. Optional behaviour ships as presets the consumer toggles: cap.presets({ tools: false }). Both exist so that a capability stays one entry in uses no matter how much it carries.

17

Orchestration

The largest composition package, and the one with the clearest internal layering: a task substrate at the bottom, a concurrent drain over it, and patterns and agents above.

The task record

A Task is a goal, a status, optional deps, and typed input/output — plus the accounting the substrate needs to be safe under concurrency: attempts, a maxAttempts retry budget, a retryLedger recording grants and budget denials against a board-wide cap, and a separate abandonment counter for work whose worker stopped renewing its lease. The status machine is enforced on write, inside the CAS, so an illegal transition never commits.

Beyond the board

GoalSeekLoop
config-driven

Re-drains the board until a judge says done. Both planAndExecute and parallelTasks are built on it.

Flow policy
work placement

Declares whether a worker's tasks belong inside the claiming request or in a session of their own. Validated and routed today; running out-of-request is groundwork.

Detached runner
outliving the turn

Skills and agents

Two ways to make behaviour first-class without writing a block: a skill is Markdown anyone can edit at runtime; an agent is a named participant you can assign work to. This is the mechanism — §04 is what it adds up to and where it stops.

Skills

A skill is a folder with a SKILL.md — YAML frontmatter plus a Markdown playbook, with supporting files reachable from the body via ${SKILL_DIR}. When it activates, the body renders into the generator's system prompt. The whole runtime lives in orchestration/src/skills: a library, a directory reader and importer, an activation store, activators, an inline-activate path, seeding, and a worker materializer.

Activation is three-tiered and runs before the main generator: a literal /<skill-name> slash match, then a local keyword scan, then a fast LLM classifier when neither decides. The matched bodies are written into session state so the main generator runs once with them already in context.

The delegation path is where skills meet the board. A skill declaring agents: installs a private task board, the taskTools capability (eight tools including addTask and completeTask), and a runBoard tool. The generator plans work as tasks with an assignee and deps, then drains the whole graph with one call. An assignee can also name one of the generator's own tools, which runs as a direct call with no model turn.

Capability or skill?

The docs draw the line cleanly: a capability is code that ships with the app, always on, structural. A skill is Markdown authored by anyone, editable at runtime, active only when matched. The two compose — the skills package itself is shipped as a capability.

Workforce

The smallest composition package in the repo at 397 lines across 8 files. Six exports: defineAgent, definePersona, createAgentRegistry, materializeAgent, agentBlock, createWorkforceCapability. The Agent and AgentRegistry contracts themselves live in core, so orchestration can type an injected materializeAgent without depending on this package.

What it does and does not yet do — and why the team layer currently lives inside skills rather than here — is §04.

Thought fabric

@thought-fabric/core is a separate namespace for cognitive-architecture primitives built on FSD rather than part of it: attention (salience scoring, relevance filtering), identity (constitution, perspective system with its own capability), and metacognition (bias detection). Its naming convention encodes category by word order — perspectiveObserve is a block, addPerspective is a helper — so the inversion signals the kind without documentation.

Memory

Four tiers, each independent and opt-in, composed by one factory and installed with one capability. Working-only is a real configuration.

TierScopeHoldsMechanics
WorkingsessionRecent observations over the current conversationSalience scoring with decay; add, evict, pin, unpin, refresh, advance. Always present.
EpisodicuserPast sessions as discrete, recallable episodesEncode, recall recent, mark consolidated, TTL culling.
SemanticuserConsolidated facts worth keepingAdd, update, reinforce, remove, query, top-N; confidence decays so stale facts rank below fresh ones.
DigestuserSummarized rollups across many sessionsPeriodic digest blocks over the lower tiers.

Two cross-cutting mechanisms give the system its shape. A hygiene janitor runs a periodic pass — semantic confidence decay, episodic eviction — on by default and disableable with hygiene: false. And every memory carries a subject: the primary user is user, and other people mentioned get their own subject, assigned at extraction and carried through every tier, so consolidation never merges facts across people.

The package ships four per-tier capability factories plus one composed createMemoryCapability, a recall-tool surface with pluggable strategies, and a context formatter per tier. It depends only on core.

Detached work

Work started with ctx.requestHost.startDetached runs in a workstream — a child session that keeps going after its originating request has returned. Where it actually runs, and whether it survives a redeploy, is entirely a function of deployment topology.

The one that bites

worker-only is the natural place to start durable jobs and the one place they silently aren't durable: the mode consumes the queue and dispatches nothing, so startDetached runs the child in the worker process and enqueues nothing. A crash loses the run outright rather than costing a retry. The docs are explicit that running it in-process there is the honest interim answer, not a guarantee — for the queue to own the work, start it from colocated or dispatch-only.

Two different waits hide behind dispose(), and only one is bounded. The in-process detached-child drain races detachedDrainTimeoutMs; separately, dispose() awaits the worker handle, which for BullMQ is a non-forced Worker.close() that waits for whatever jobs the process has claimed, with no framework budget over it. Size the platform's kill timeout for the longest job, not for detachedDrainTimeoutMs.

Refactor lenses

Nine tensions the inventory surfaces. None of these is a bug report — each is a place where the code and the stated grounding pull in slightly different directions, and where a decision would be worth more than another patch.

A tenth sits in §04 rather than here, because it needs the stack drawn to make sense: workforce is the one layer specified top-down, and the question is whether a team is a new primitive or a composition of parts that already work.

1 · The two mega-packages

core (34k lines, 160 files) and engine (42k, 167) are 43% of all source. core/src/index.ts is 443 lines of nothing but export statements across a dozen subdirectories — blocks, flow, capability, graph, items, prompt, models, helpers, adapters, schema, tools, utility, resource-template.

The precedent for splitting already exists: contracts was carved out of core and re-exported so consumers never noticed. Twelve subpath exports on core suggest the seams are already drawn.

Is core one package with twelve subpaths, or three packages? What did carving out contracts actually buy, and does that argument extend?
2 · Orchestration is two packages sharing an entry point

Its own index.ts header says "Three layers behind one entry point" and then names two — the task substrate and the skills runtime — plus a task-board subpath. The two already have different runtime constraints: ./tasks is published browser-safe, while skills reach node:fs, and the lease-renewal seam is deliberately kept off the browser-safe subpath because it needs node:async_hooks.

That is a package boundary being enforced by comments and subpath discipline rather than by a package boundary.

Do skills and the task substrate belong together because they compose, or are they together because they arrived together?
3 · Five mechanisms for "pick the next thing"

The router block kind. utility.keyedRouter (record lookup, no model). utility.intentRouter (classifier + router). Task dispatchers — fifo, priority, topological, event, classifier. Skill activation's three tiers — slash match, keyword scan, LLM classifier.

Some of these are genuinely different problems (selecting a block vs selecting a task vs selecting a playbook). Others look like the same decision at different altitudes, each with its own vocabulary.

Which of the five are the same shape? Is "classifier" the same concept in a task dispatcher and in skill activation, and if so should it be one implementation?
4 · The architecture overview is measurably stale

docs/architecture/overview.md opens its package section with "Six packages with strict dependency boundaries" and lists eight names. There are 27. Its dependency graph shows five nodes. It does not mention orchestration, patterns, memory, tools, workforce, or any host or store adapter.

Tenet 1 is explicit that a doc/code mismatch is itself incoherence, and that the move is to resolve it deliberately rather than rank one over the other silently. This one has a clear answer — the code is right — but it has not been made.

Is the overview meant to be a complete map or a 30-second on-ramp? If the latter, say so in it, and point at something that is complete.
5 · Superseded paths that outlived their supersession

24 @deprecated markers across the framework packages. patterns/src/shared/legacy-plan-types.ts plus a legacyWorkerAdapter and a comment recording that the runtime plan-emit helpers were retired. Two generator loop implementations side by side — the FSD-owned step loop and the legacy SDK-driven generate({ maxSteps }) path.

The generator pair has a stated reason (fallback model groups and older custom adapters genuinely need the old path). The others read more like residue. Tenet 3 says a change that supersedes a path deletes it in the same change — old and new side by side is how incoherence starts.

Which of these have a removal condition someone could actually check, and which are just still there? Naming the condition is most of the work.
6 · Declared surface that isn't wired through yet

Flow policy lets a board declare a worker's tasks as work belonging outside the claiming request. The docs say plainly that such a worker is "validated, routed, and then still run inline" — the declaration is groundwork. rlm carries a header saying it has no consumers in the repo and asking not to be deleted because a demo is a roadmap item.

Both are honest, both are documented, and both are the exact shape tenet 3 warns about — a contract you can't take back, taken on before there is a caller.

Does honest documentation of a not-yet-real capability discharge the cost of carrying it, or defer it? A date or a triggering condition would turn each of these from a standing exception into a decision.
7 · Three tiers for "reusable behaviour", with a fuzzy border

A utility is a single block. A pattern is a multi-block sequencer. A capability is a bundle installed via uses. The stated line between the first two is crisp — but intentRouter and sessionTitleGenerator are sequencers living in the utility tier, which is the pattern tier's definition.

Meanwhile anything that carries context or tools can be expressed as a capability instead of either, which means most reusable behaviour has two or three plausible homes.

Is the utility/pattern line about block count or about who composes it? A rule stated as "single block" that two of eleven members break is either the wrong rule or has two exceptions worth naming.
8 · Persistence is an exception list on the item registry

Of 18 item types: status is always transient; state_change is transient in prod and persistent in dev; resource_change is transient by default; component is a keyed upsert per (requestId, key); state_snapshot is stripped from the request log and side-channelled to the checkpoint store; generator_step is persistent but replay-only and never in a GET.

That is six distinct persistence behaviours encoded per type, alongside a separate three-category visibility axis that is modelled explicitly.

Visibility got a resolution function and three named categories. Should persistence get the same treatment — a declared axis rather than a column of exceptions?
9 · Three things are called a host

RequestHost in core (the detached-work seam, startDetached). InboundTransportHost in engine (what an adapter's createBindings receives). And the host-adapter packages — node, next, vercel, bullmq — which are what a deployment calls its host.

They are genuinely three different things, and the detached-work topology matrix has to talk about all three at once to explain a single behaviour, which is why that table is as hard to read as it is.

Worth checking whether the naming collision is causing the explanation cost, or merely accompanying it.

Where the leverage probably is

Four of the nine (1, 2, 5, 7) are the same underlying question at different scales: what is a unit of the framework, and what makes something graduate from one tier to the next? The philosophy has an answer for whether a thing should exist (tenet 3) and an answer for whether it coheres (tenet 1), but not an explicit answer for where it lives once it does exist. Packages, tiers, and subpaths are all currently doing that job, and they disagree with each other in exactly the places above.

The other five are more local: a stale doc with a known correct answer (4), documented-but-unwired surface needing a removal condition (6), an exception list that wants to become an axis (8), a naming collision worth measuring (9), and a routing vocabulary worth unifying or deliberately splitting (3).

One caution on reading this

Line counts here measure mass, not health. engine being the largest package is what you would expect from the package that owns execution, persistence, streaming, and transport — its size is evidence about where complexity lives, not evidence that it is misplaced. The tensions above are drawn from stated contracts disagreeing with observed code, not from size.
